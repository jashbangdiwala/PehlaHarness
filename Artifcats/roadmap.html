Roadmap

Everything after Phase 1

One block per phase: what you build, why it matters, the test that says you are done, the one thing to read first and the questions you should be able to answer afterwards.

Read only the block you are about to start. Each phase gets its own step-by-step guide when you reach it, the way Phase 1 has one now.

The order

Evals come second on purpose. Every later phase means changing something and proving nothing broke, and you cannot prove that without evals. After Phase 2 you can take Cost, Security and Memory in any order.

Watch the harness grow

The whole system, with a switch for each phase. Start at 0 and click upward.

2

Evals

An eval is a repeatable test of how the agent behaves. You build the test set, the thing that runs it and the things that grade it.

You build
A golden set: 20 real messages, each with the outcome that should happen. A runner that replays them through the EA and keeps the traces. Graders: code checks where code can decide (was log_food called with meal="lunch"?) and an LLM judge where it cannot (was the reply brief and useful?). One command that scores version A of a prompt against version B.
Why it matters
An agent that works when you try it by hand has told you very little. Models vary from run to run, and a prompt change that fixes one case can break three others. Most teams are weak at evals, which makes this one of the most valuable skills on the list.
Exit test
One command prints a scorecard comparing two versions of the EA.
Read first
Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar's evals FAQ. Their conversation on Lenny's Podcast covers the same ideas in an hour of audio.
Got it when
You can explain why you read traces before you pick metrics, how you show an LLM judge can be trusted, the difference between a regression eval and a capability eval, and why one run is not a result.
3

Cost

You already log token counts for every model call. This phase turns them into money, then cuts the bill without lowering the eval score.

You build
A cost ledger from the usage data in your events. A budget cap enforced in the gateway. Prompt caching, so repeated context is billed at a discount. Routing: easy messages go to a cheaper model, with a small decision model (Jev, see Phase 6) making the call.
Why it matters
Cost per task decides whether an AI feature survives a budget review. There are only a few levers, and with your ledger and your evals you can measure each one.
Exit test
A report showing cost per task went down while the eval score held.
Read first
OpenRouter's own pages on prompt caching and provider selection. You already run on it.
Got it when
You can break one task's bill into input, output and cached tokens, explain why a cache hit needs the start of the prompt to stay the same, and rank your three biggest cost levers using numbers from your own ledger.
4

Security

You attack your own assistant, write down what worked and fix it. The policy check from Phase 1 grows into a real control.

You build
A threat model: a short document saying what can go wrong, who could cause it and what stops it. Approvals in the policy layer, so risky tools wait for your yes. A red-team agent that attacks the EA with planted instructions and bad input. An attack suite inside your evals, so a change that weakens security fails the same way a broken feature does.
Why it matters
Prompt injection has no complete fix. An agent that reads untrusted text, holds private data and can send things out can be turned against its owner. Designing around that limit is the core of AI security work.
Exit test
The attack suite runs in the eval gate. A findings document lists each attack that landed and its fix.
Read first
Simon Willison's The lethal trifecta, then the OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications. For the government view, Careful Adoption of Agentic AI Services from CISA and partner agencies (May 2026). MITRE ATLAS is the reference catalogue of attack techniques.
Got it when
You can explain why injection cannot be filtered out, point to the three legs of the trifecta inside your own EA, say which tool calls need your approval and why, and walk through one attack your red-team agent landed.
5

Memory

After Phase 1 the EA remembers ten turns and whatever its tools saved. This phase gives it a memory worth the name.

You build
A store for things worth keeping. Retrieval: finding the few stored items that matter for the current message and adding them to the context. A nightly job that tidies the store by merging duplicates and dropping what is stale.
Why it matters
Context is limited and you pay for all of it. Deciding what to keep, what to fetch and what to forget is a design problem every serious agent has. Retrieval is also the most common pattern in company AI systems, where it goes by the name RAG.
Exit test
Recall evals pass, for example "what did I eat last week?"
Read first
LangChain's RAG From Scratch series: short videos with a notebook each, one idea at a time. Your earlier agent-wikis retrieval research applies here too.
Got it when
You can show retrieval failing separately from generation, measure recall on your own questions, and explain what the nightly job keeps, merges and forgets.
6

Game agent

A second agent, nothing like the first. It plays 2048 in a browser. 2048 works well because it looks good in a demo, the board can be read as text, and the score is the eval.

You build
A sandboxed browser the agent controls. A state reader that turns the board into text. A planner (the LLM) that sets strategy, and a mover (Jev) that picks each move.
What Jev is
TypeSafe's decision model. You give it the situation and a typed question, such as "which of these four moves?", and it returns an answer with a probability. It writes no text. That makes it cheap and fast for the many small decisions inside a loop.
Why it matters
It shows your core is general: a new agent, with no changes to the EA. It also teaches the split between fast, cheap decisions and slow, expensive reasoning, which is where most cost savings come from.
Exit test
A demo video and a table: score and cost per game, with Jev and without.
Read first
OpenRouter's Jev guide, then TypeSafe's docs.
Got it when
You can sort every step of your game loop into "decision" or "generation", explain calibration (answers given 0.9 should be right about nine times in ten), say why you pin the model version before tuning thresholds, and show the cost per game both ways.
7

Multi-agent

Agents working together, and an honest measurement of whether that helped.

You build
An orchestrator that hands work to other agents and collects what they return. The red-team agent from Phase 4 running continuously against the EA.
Why it matters
Multi-agent designs are fashionable and often worse than one good agent. They use more tokens and have more ways to fail. Knowing when they pay off, with your own numbers, sets you apart from people who only know the diagram.
Exit test
An ADR with numbers: where several agents beat one, and where they lost.
Read first
Two pieces that disagree, back to back: Anthropic's How we built our multi-agent research system and Cognition's Don't Build Multi-Agents.
Got it when
You can argue both sides, state the token multiplier you measured, and say exactly what a sub-agent hands back to its parent and what it must not.
8

Enterprise pack

Documents, no code. You describe the harness the way a regulated company would need it described before it could be approved.

You build
A reference architecture. A control mapping from what your harness does to the frameworks such a company follows: NIST AI RMF, ISO/IEC 42001, the EU AI Act and, for banks, SR 11-7. A card for each agent: what it does, what it may touch, who owns it. An incident playbook: the kill switch, rolling back a prompt, the review afterwards.
Why it matters
This is the translation between an engineer and a risk committee. Most of an architect's output is documents like these.
Exit test
A set of documents an architecture review board would accept.
Read first
The NIST AI Risk Management Framework and its Generative AI Profile, linked from the same page.
Got it when
You can map one harness control to one framework line, draw your reference architecture from memory, and explain who signs off on a prompt change at a bank and what evidence they need.

Models

The Lab below shows how a model works inside. This track is the job people do in practice: take an existing open model from Hugging Face, adapt it, prove it is better and publish it. Each step reuses what the one before it built.

Read first
Hugging Face's free LLM Course, then its smol-course for fine-tuning.
Rules
Never publish raw EA logs. Public training data is synthetic or generated by the red team, then scrubbed and read by you. Every published model has a model card: what it is for, what it was trained on, its eval numbers, its known failures and its licence. Every training run is logged with its GPU hours and cost. GPU spend is its own budget, outside the $20 a month for model calls.
Got it when
You can say when not to fine-tune, spot overfitting on a loss curve, explain what LoRA trains and defend your train/test split against leakage.

Open items

  • Jev version and price. Access is settled: an OpenRouter key is enough. Versions 1.13 and 1.14 are both listed. Pin one when you reach Phase 3 and read the price off its model page then.
  • Jev and images. It accepts images, but OpenRouter marks that as an early feature that runs in a slower mode. The plan still reads the 2048 board as text.
  • Serving for M4. A classifier or embedding model runs on the MacBook's CPU. A tuned LLM needs a hosted endpoint billed by the hour. Price it when you get there.
  • Tools for the Models track. Training libraries and GPU rental are not chosen yet. Pick them at M1.
  • Framework editions. Confirm the current versions of the OWASP, NIST, ISO and EU documents when you reach Phases 4 and 8.
